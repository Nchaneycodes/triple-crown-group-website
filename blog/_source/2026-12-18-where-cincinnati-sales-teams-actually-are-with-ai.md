**Quick answer:** In our field work with sales and marketing organizations across Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky, most companies sit at stage two or three of five, informal individual usage, or licenses purchased without adoption. Very few have a single documented, measured workflow. The transition from informal usage to one standardized workflow is the hardest and most valuable step, and it is where almost all of the available return sits.

These are observations from our own engagements and assessments, not survey data. They are offered as a diagnostic, not a statistic.

## The five stages

### Stage 1: Nothing

No tools, no usage, no policy. Rare now, and usually found at businesses under 15 people in relationship-driven trades where the owner has consciously decided not to engage.

**What we see:** often less of a problem than expected. A ten-person contractor with a working process and no bottleneck is not behind. They are fine.

**What moves them:** a specific pain, a retiring employee who knows everything, a reporting burden that has become intolerable, a customer requiring faster quotes.

### Stage 2: Informal individual usage

Two or three people use AI regularly on their own initiative. Nobody has documented anything. Nobody knows who is using what with which data. No policy exists.

**This is the most common stage we encounter.**

**What it looks like:** a marketing coordinator drafting everything in a personal ChatGPT account. A sales rep with a research routine nobody else knows about. An owner using it for email. All of them productive, none of it compounding.

**The risk nobody has assessed:** company and customer data flowing into consumer-tier tools with no agreement, no admin control, and no retention policy. This is happening at nearly every company in stage 2, and leadership is usually unaware of the extent.

**What moves them to stage 3, or better, straight to stage 4:** honestly, the best move from stage 2 is to skip stage 3 entirely. Buy business-tier licenses, write the two-page policy, then immediately standardize one workflow. Companies that buy licenses without standardizing land in stage 3, which is a worse place than stage 2 because it has a budget line attached.

### Stage 3: Licensed but unadopted

Licenses purchased, often company-wide. Utilization sits somewhere between 15% and 40%. Leadership believes they have "done AI." Nobody is measuring anything.

**What it looks like:** a rollout email, a recorded webinar, and forty seats where twelve people log in monthly.

**Why it happens:** the purchase was treated as the implementation. No workflow was defined, no task was removed, no manager reinforced anything, and no baseline was ever taken.

**The characteristic symptom:** when you ask what changed, nobody can name anything specific. When you ask what people use it for, the answer is "writing emails, I think."

**What moves them:** stop buying and start standardizing. One workflow, documented, trained hands-on with the manager present, with an existing task explicitly removed, and adoption measured at 30, 60, and 90 days. This usually costs less than the licenses already purchased.

### Stage 4: One workflow standardized and measured

A single documented workflow (usually research briefs, call capture, or reporting) used by most of the team, with adoption tracked and a baseline metric that moved.

**What it looks like:** a prompt library people actually reference. A workflow new hires are trained on. A number leadership can cite. Someone who has customized the prompt for themselves.

**Why this stage matters so much:** the first standardized workflow is where the difficulty is. It requires defining a process, changing a habit, and measuring something, three organizational muscles most companies have not exercised. Every subsequent workflow is meaningfully easier because those muscles now exist.

**What moves them to stage 5:** naming an internal owner with protected time, and adding the second workflow.

### Stage 5: Multiple workflows with an internal owner

Two or more standardized workflows, a named owner with protected time, a maintained prompt library, AI in the onboarding path, and a review cadence.

**What it looks like:** the company can add a new workflow in about eight weeks without outside help. New hires are trained on AI workflows as standard practice, not as an add-on. Someone notices when a model update degrades output quality.

**Very few of the companies we assess are here at first contact.** Those that are usually got there through one determined internal person rather than through a program.

## What we consistently see, regardless of stage

**Leadership overestimates where they are by about one stage.** Almost universally. The stage-3 company believes it is at stage 4. The gap is usually visible in whether anyone can name a number that moved.

**The data problem is bigger than expected.** CRM duplicate rates of 15 to 35% are common in mid-sized B2B companies here. This does not block drafting and research workflows, but it blocks everything grounded in account history.

**Managers are the constraint, not reps.** Where adoption fails, tracing it back almost always leads to a manager who never used the tool and never referenced it in a team ritual. Rep resistance is a symptom.

**The gap between industries is smaller than expected.** A manufacturer in Florence and an agency in OTR face substantially similar adoption problems. The workflows differ; the human dynamics do not.

**Nobody has a policy.** In stage 2 and 3 companies, a written AI use policy is present maybe one time in five. This is the cheapest and most-skipped item in the entire category.

## Diagnosing your own stage

Four questions:

1. **Can you name a specific workflow that everyone on the team does the same way with AI?** No → stage 3 or below.
2. **Do you have a number that moved, measured the same way before and after?** No → stage 3 or below.
3. **Is there a written policy people have acknowledged?** No → your risk is higher than you think regardless of stage.
4. **Would a new hire be trained on an AI workflow in their first month?** Yes → stage 4 or 5.

If you answered no to 1 and 2, you are earlier than you believe, and that is genuinely fine. Most companies are. The teams that get ahead are not the ones with the most tools. They are the ones that standardized one thing and measured it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many small businesses are actually using AI?**
Many have licenses; few have standardized, measured workflows. The second number is the one that predicts outcomes.

**What does AI maturity look like for a sales team?**
Five stages: nothing, informal usage, licensed but unadopted, one workflow standardized, multiple workflows with an owner.

**How do you move from experimenting to actually using AI?**
Standardize one workflow, document it, train hands-on with the manager present, remove an existing task, measure at 90 days.

**Is stage 3 worse than stage 2?**
In one sense yes, because you are paying for it and leadership believes the problem is solved, which makes it harder to get attention and budget for the work that actually produces the change.

**How long does it take to get from stage 2 to stage 4?**
Ninety days with focus. Most companies take a year or more because they attempt several workflows simultaneously and complete none.
