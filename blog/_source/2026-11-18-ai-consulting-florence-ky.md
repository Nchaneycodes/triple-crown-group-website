**Quick answer:** For a Florence-area industrial company, the right first AI pilot is account and plant research briefs, not quoting, not anything custom. It removes prep work reps dislike, changes nothing about how they sell, and shows value in week one. That first win is what buys permission for the harder, higher-value projects later. Ninety days, roughly $12,000 to $25,000.

Florence sits at the center of Boone County's manufacturing and logistics concentration, Mazak's headquarters, the CVG corridor, and a dense base of industrial suppliers, distributors, and 3PLs. The sales organizations at these companies share a profile: tenured reps, technical buyers, long cycles, and a healthy skepticism about new software.

Getting the first pilot right matters more here than in most markets, because a failed first attempt at a company like this does not get a second one for two years.

## Why the obvious first project is the wrong one

Ask a leadership team at an industrial company where AI would help most and the answer is usually quoting. They are right about the value. Quote turnaround is genuinely where the money is.

They are wrong about the sequence, for three reasons:

**Catalog data.** A quoting agent grounded in your catalog requires structured catalog data. Most industrial companies do not have it in usable form. That cleanup is 30 to 50% of the project, it is invisible to the sales team, and it takes months. You will spend your political capital on infrastructure nobody can see.

**Trust.** A quoting tool touches money. A rep who gets one wrong number from it stops using it permanently, and tells everyone. That risk is acceptable once the team believes AI works. It is not acceptable as an introduction.

**Adoption difficulty.** Quoting is a habit that has existed for decades. Changing it is a hard behavior change, and hard behavior changes should not be first.

## What to do instead

**Account and plant research briefs.** Before every meaningful visit or call, the rep gets a one-page brief: the operation, recent capital and expansion announcements, current suppliers where known, their history with your company, and what was committed on the last visit.

Why this works as a first project at an industrial company specifically:

- It removes work, adds none
- It requires zero change to how a rep sells
- Tenured reps see it as respecting their expertise rather than replacing it
- Value is visible in the first week
- It costs $8,000 to $15,000 including training, not $30,000
- It produces a story the team tells each other, which is what actually spreads adoption

## The 90-day sequence

**Weeks 1 to 3: Assessment.** Shadow four to six reps. Time logs from a broader sample. Inventory what tools exist and who actually uses them. Baseline the number you will measure, hours spent on pre-visit prep per rep per week.

**Weeks 4 to 5: Manager-first pilot.** The sales manager and one respected senior rep use the brief workflow. Nobody else. They find the problems and fix the prompt. The version the team eventually sees was built by their own manager, not by a consultant, and that matters at a company like this.

**Weeks 6 to 8: Team rollout.** In person, hands on keyboards, on real accounts. Cap the room at 15. The manager announces what prep step goes away. Everyone leaves with the prompt.

**Weeks 9 to 12: Reinforcement and measurement.** Manager references briefs in pipeline reviews. Individual follow-up with anyone who stopped. They usually have one specific fixable blocker. At week 12, re-measure prep hours and check whether anyone has customized the prompt.

**Cost:** $12,000 to $25,000 including licensing for a team of 10 to 25.

## What comes after, in order

If the first pilot works, the sequence that follows:

1. **Catalog and product data cleanup.** Now you have credibility to spend on infrastructure. Often AI-assisted, 6 to 12 weeks, $8,000 to $25,000.
2. **Quote or cross-reference agent.** The main event, now that the data supports it and the team trusts the category. $15,000 to $30,000.
3. **RFQ and specification response.** Expanding into the document-heavy work.
4. **Reporting and roll-up automation.** Manager-side time recovery.

Companies that follow this order get to the quoting agent about a month later than companies that start with it, and they actually get there, which is the difference.

## The tenured rep question

Industrial sales teams in Boone County skew experienced. A 22-year rep who knows which plant manager cares about changeover time is not going to be persuaded by a slide about digital transformation.

What works with that rep:

- Give them back the two hours a week they spend assembling prep material
- Say plainly that nothing about how they sell is changing
- Put them in the pilot rather than saving them for last
- Let them break it and complain, and then fix what they broke

What does not work: efficiency language, modernization framing, or anything that implies the current method is deficient. They hear headcount, and given how these things have gone at other companies, that is a reasonable inference.
