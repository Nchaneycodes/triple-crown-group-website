**Quick answer:** Keep brand voice by documenting it from your own best content (including a list of words and constructions to ban) putting that guide in every prompt, and having a human do a substantive edit rather than a proofread. The most reliable workflow is human point of view, AI expansion, human edit. Asking AI for a complete draft from a topic produces the flat, recognizable output that damages credibility.

The failure mode here is not that AI writes badly. It writes competently, which is worse, because competent and generic is exactly what nobody wants to read from a company they are considering paying.

## Why unedited AI content reads the way it does

The tells are structural, and once you see them you cannot unsee them:

- **Uniform rhythm.** Every paragraph three to four sentences. Every sentence similar length. Human writing varies. It has short ones.
- **Everything in threes.** Three benefits, three challenges, three considerations. Reality is rarely so tidy.
- **Hedged conclusions.** "Ultimately, the right approach depends on your specific needs." True, useless, and instantly recognizable.
- **Signposting phrases.** "In today's rapidly evolving landscape." "It's important to note that." "Let's dive in."
- **Specificity absence.** Numbers, names, dates, and real examples are where credibility lives, and generic drafts contain none because the model does not know yours.
- **No opinion.** The model is trained toward balance. Balanced content is unmemorable content, and it does not get cited by AI assistants either. Vague advice is structurally uncitable.

Readers may not name these, but they register them, and the impression is that nobody at the company cared enough to write it themselves.

## Build a voice guide from your own writing

Do not write a voice guide from adjectives. "Professional yet approachable" is meaningless to a model and to a human.

**Build it from artifacts:**

1. **Collect your five best pieces.** The ones that got responses, that people quoted back to you, that sound like the company at its best.
2. **Have AI analyze them.** "Analyze these five pieces. Describe the voice in terms of sentence structure, vocabulary level, use of first and second person, how arguments are constructed, what rhetorical moves recur, and what is conspicuously absent."
3. **Edit the output.** It will get some things wrong. Fix them. This is now the beginning of a usable guide.
4. **Add a ban list.** This is the highest-value section and the one everyone skips. Specific words and constructions your brand does not use. Ours might include: "leverage," "seamless," "game-changing," "in today's landscape," "unlock," "empower," "revolutionize," em-dash-heavy sentence structures, and any sentence beginning "As a company, we believe."
5. **Add three examples of each.** A sentence in your voice, and the same idea in a voice that is not yours. Contrast teaches better than description.

Keep it to two pages. Put the whole thing in every content prompt.

## The workflow that actually works

**Step 1, Human decides the point of view.** What is the argument? What do we believe that others in our category do not? This cannot be delegated. A piece without a point of view is not worth producing regardless of who writes it.

**Step 2. AI does the research.** Gather sources, competitive positions, data, counterarguments. This is where AI is genuinely strongest and where most teams under-use it.

**Step 3, Human writes the opening and the outline.** The first 100 words set the voice for everything after. Writing them yourself and providing them as an example produces dramatically better continuation than any voice description.

**Step 4, AI expands the outline.** With the voice guide, the opening, and the research in the prompt.

**Step 5. Human does a substantive edit.** Not proofreading. Substantive editing means: cutting the sections that say nothing, adding the specific examples only you have, inserting the opinions the model hedged, breaking the uniform rhythm, and removing everything on the ban list.

Budget real time for step 5. A good edit takes 30 to 45 minutes on a 1,200-word piece. If your team is spending five minutes, you are publishing AI output with a human timestamp on it, and readers will know.

**Step 6, Add what only you have.** A client example. A number from your own work. A thing you got wrong last year. Original material is what makes content worth reading and what makes it citable by AI assistants, models reward uniqueness because nobody else has it.

## Where AI is genuinely better than a human first draft

Being fair about this:

- **Structural variants.** Adapting one piece into an email, five social posts, and a newsletter section. Mechanical, tedious, and AI does it well.
- **Research synthesis.** Reading twenty sources and extracting what matters.
- **Technical explanation at multiple levels.** Same concept for a specialist and a generalist audience.
- **Second-pass editing.** "Find every sentence in this draft that makes a claim without evidence." Genuinely useful and under-used.
- **Headline and subject line variants.** Volume matters here and quality per unit does not.
- **Translation and localization first passes.**

## Quality standards worth writing down

Publish nothing that fails these:

1. **Does it contain at least one thing only we could have written?** A client story, our own data, a specific position.
2. **Would a knowledgeable reader learn something?** If it summarizes what is already widely known, it does not need to exist.
3. **Does it take a position?** If every paragraph could have been written by any competitor, it is filler.
4. **Are there specifics?** Numbers, names, dates, real examples.
5. **Does it sound like us?** Read it aloud. This catches more than any checklist.
6. **Is anything factually asserted that we have not verified?** AI produces confident, plausible, wrong statements. Verify every factual claim, especially numbers and attributions.

That last one has ended careers. Check the facts.

## Volume versus quality

The temptation with AI content is volume. Resist it.

**For traditional SEO,** thin content dilutes topical authority and increasingly triggers quality demotions.

**For AEO,** it is worse. AI assistants extract from substantive pages with direct answers and specific data. Thin content is not extracted at all, so publishing a hundred shallow pages produces zero citations while diluting the signal from your good ones.

**The better ratio:** twenty substantive pieces a year beats two hundred shallow ones, on every measure that matters. Use the capacity AI gives you to make each piece deeper (more research, more examples, more original data) rather than to make more of them.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you keep brand voice when using AI?**
A voice guide built from your own best content with a specific ban list, included in every prompt, plus a substantive human edit.

**Can people tell when content is AI-generated?**
Increasingly yes, for unedited output. The tells are structural. Substantive editing removes most of them.

**Should AI write the first draft?**
It works better as a research and expansion tool than as a blank-page writer. Human point of view and opening, AI expansion, human edit.

**Does AI content hurt SEO?**
Low-quality content hurts SEO regardless of origin. Search engines have consistently said the standard is helpfulness and quality, not authorship. In practice, unedited AI content tends to be low quality, which is why the correlation exists.

**How much editing is enough?**
If the piece contains something only you could have written and it sounds like you when read aloud, enough. If you cannot tell it from a competitor's post, not enough.
