**Quick answer:** Hire a consultant when you need to go from zero to working inside a quarter and then hand it off. Hire an agency when you want ongoing output produced for you and are not trying to build internal capability. Hire in-house when AI work will be continuous, central, and large enough to justify $95,000 to $140,000 fully loaded. Most Cincinnati-area SMBs under 150 employees should start with a consultant and hire later, if at all.

This decision gets made badly because the three options are usually compared on price rather than on what you end up owning.

## What each option actually produces

| | Consultant | Agency | In-house hire |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary output** | A plan, a trained team, working systems | Ongoing deliverables | Continuous internal capability |
| **You end up owning** | Capability | Output | Both, eventually |
| **Time to first value** | 2 to 8 weeks | 2 to 4 weeks | 3 to 6 months |
| **First-year cost (SMB)** | $25,000 to $60,000 | $36,000 to $120,000 | $95,000 to $140,000 |
| **Ends cleanly?** | Yes | Yes | No |
| **Best when** | You need to change how your team works | You need volume produced | AI is core to the business |

## The consultant case

**Buy a consultant when the problem is that your team's workflow needs to change.** That is a different problem than "we need more content produced," and it is the more common one at companies with existing sales and marketing staff.

The strength of the model: you get concentrated senior attention for a defined period, and at the end your own people can do the thing. The engagement is designed to end. That is a feature.

The weakness: it depends on your team having capacity to absorb the change. A consultant cannot make adoption happen in an organization where nobody has ten hours a month and no manager will reinforce anything.

**Realistic first-year cost for a Cincinnati SMB:** $25,000 to $60,000 for audit, one or two builds, and training. Plus licensing.

## The agency case

**Buy an agency when you need production volume and do not need internal capability.** Content, campaign management, creative, media buying. AI-enabled agencies produce more per dollar than they did three years ago, and for a company with no marketing headcount, this is often the right answer.

The strength: output starts fast and continues without internal effort.

The weakness: the capability lives with them. Three years in, you have a body of work and no internal ability to do it yourself, plus a switching cost that grows every month. That is a legitimate trade, but make it consciously.

**Realistic cost:** $3,000 to $10,000/month for a small business retainer.

## The in-house case

**Hire when AI work is continuous, central, and there is enough of it to fill a role.** For a 40-person company, there usually is not. For a 200-person company with a real revenue operations function, there usually is.

The Cincinnati market reality: a competent RevOps or marketing operations person with genuine AI capability runs **$85,000 to $115,000 base**, which is roughly **$95,000 to $140,000 fully loaded** with benefits and taxes. That is real money, and the search takes three to five months in this market because the supply of people who can both build and drive adoption is thin.

The strength: continuous, context-rich, and compounding. Nobody understands your business better than someone inside it.

The weakness: single point of failure, long ramp, and the risk that you hire someone strong at tooling and weak at change management, which is the more common half of the job and the harder one to interview for.

**A common intermediate move:** promote internally. The person on your team who has already been experimenting with AI on their own is frequently a better bet than an external hire, because they have the organizational trust that makes adoption possible. Give them 25% of their time formally protected and a consultant to accelerate them. This works more often than it should, given how rarely it is tried.

## The hybrid that usually wins

For most SMBs in the 25 to 150 employee range, the sequence that produces the best outcome:

1. **Quarter 1:** Consultant runs an audit and one build. Cost: $12,000 to $25,000. You learn what is actually worth doing, which is worth the fee independent of anything built.
2. **Quarter 2:** Consultant trains the team and hands off. Internal owner identified, usually an existing employee with 20 to 25% of their time protected. Cost: $8,000 to $20,000.
3. **Quarter 3 onward:** Internal owner runs it. Consultant on a light retainer ($1,500 to $4,000/month) for the harder builds and quarterly check-ins.
4. **Year 2:** Decide whether the volume justifies a dedicated hire. Now you can answer that question with data instead of guessing.

Total first-year outlay: roughly $35,000 to $65,000 including the light retainer, versus $120,000+ for a hire who spends their first quarter figuring out what you already would have learned in step 1.

## How to decide in ten minutes

Answer these:

- **Will there be meaningful AI work in three years?** If clearly yes, plan toward in-house eventually. If unclear, consultant.
- **Is the problem "we need more output" or "we need to work differently"?** Output → agency. Different → consultant.
- **Does anyone internal have 8 to 10 protected hours a week?** No → agency, or fix that first, because a consultant will fail without it.
- **Do you have a manager who will reinforce a behavior change?** No → nothing will stick regardless of who you hire. Solve that first.
- **Is your budget under $30,000?** Consultant, scoped narrowly. Do not try to spread it across two models.

## The failure mode nobody warns you about

Hiring an agency to solve an adoption problem. It happens constantly: a company's sales team is not using AI, so they hire an agency, which produces excellent AI-assisted output *for* them. Two years later the sales team still does not use AI and now there is a monthly invoice.

Agencies are good at production. They are structurally not incentivized to change how your people work, because that reduces what you need from them. Match the model to the actual problem.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I hire an AI consultant or an employee?**
Consultant if you need working systems inside a quarter and then a handoff. Employee if AI work will be continuous and central for years. In Cincinnati, the fully loaded cost of the hire is roughly two to four times a first-year consulting engagement.

**What is the difference between an AI consultant and an AI agency?**
Consultants build your capability and the engagement ends. Agencies produce output and the engagement continues. Neither is better; they solve different problems.

**Can I do this without outside help?**
Yes, if someone internal has 8 to 10 protected hours a week and the authority to change how the team works. Technical knowledge is rarely the constraint. Protected time and authority almost always are.

**What if I hire a consultant and my team ignores them?**
That is a leadership problem and it will not be solved by the consultant. Before engaging anyone, confirm that a manager will attend the training, use the tool themselves, and reference it in an existing team ritual.
