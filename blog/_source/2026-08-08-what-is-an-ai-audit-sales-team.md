**Quick answer:** An AI audit is a two-to-four-week assessment that maps where your sales team's hours actually go, identifies which of those hours AI can realistically return, and ranks the opportunities by effort and impact. The deliverable is a prioritized plan with cost estimates, including an explicit list of things *not* worth doing. It should cost $3,000 to $8,000 for a small or mid-sized team.

Most companies buy AI tools before they understand their own workflow. Then they wonder why usage collapses after week three. An audit inverts that order. It is unglamorous and it is the highest-return two weeks most sales organizations will spend on this.

Here is what actually happens inside one.

## Week 1: Finding out where the hours go

The premise of the entire exercise is that leadership's mental model of how reps spend their week is usually wrong, not because anyone is lying, but because nobody has measured it.

**Rep shadowing.** Sitting with three to six reps across performance tiers for a few hours each, watching the actual sequence of work. Not asking them to describe it. Watching. The gap between the described process and the observed process is where most of the opportunity lives.

**Time diaries.** A lightweight one-week log from a broader sample. Categories, not narratives: prospect research, CRM entry, proposal building, internal reporting, email drafting, live selling, meeting prep, follow-up.

**System inventory.** Every tool the team touches, who has licenses, and (critically) what percentage of paid seats show meaningful usage. Dormant licenses are common and represent recoverable spend before a dollar of new budget is committed.

**CRM data pull.** Record counts, duplicate rates, field completion rates, note quality, activity logging consistency. This determines whether anything intelligent can be built on top of your data or whether cleanup has to come first.

**Leadership interviews.** What leadership believes the bottleneck is, and what they would spend to fix it. Documented separately, because it frequently disagrees with the observed data. That disagreement is worth surfacing.

## Week 2: Quantifying the opportunities

Every observed activity gets scored on four dimensions:

| Dimension | Question | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | How many hours per rep per week? | Hours |
| Repeatability | Is the process consistent enough to automate? | 1 to 5 |
| AI fit | Can current tools actually do this well? | 1 to 5 |
| Change difficulty | How hard will adoption be? | 1 to 5 |

That fourth column is where most audits get lazy and most projects die. A task that is high-volume, highly repeatable, and technically easy is still a bad first project if it requires reps to abandon a habit they have held for eleven years. Sequencing around adoption difficulty matters more than sequencing around technical difficulty.

The output is a scored matrix. Typical findings for a B2B sales team look something like:

- **Pre-call research: 5 to 9 hours/rep/week.** High volume, highly repeatable, strong AI fit, low change difficulty. Usually the first project.
- **CRM note entry and activity logging: 3 to 5 hours/rep/week.** High volume, moderate repeatability, strong AI fit, *high* change difficulty because it means changing how reps run calls.
- **Proposal and quote drafting: 2 to 6 hours/rep/week.** Moderate volume, high repeatability if you have a coherent product catalog, strong AI fit. Often the highest-dollar opportunity.
- **Leadership roll-up reporting: 4 to 12 hours/week concentrated in managers.** Low rep impact, very high manager impact, extremely repeatable. Often the easiest visible win because the person who benefits is the person who approves the project.
- **Live selling and relationship work.** Deliberately excluded. Flagging what AI should *not* touch is part of a credible audit.

## Weeks 3 to 4: Validation and delivery

**Validation sessions.** Findings go back to the reps who were observed before they go to leadership. Two reasons: reps catch errors in how work was characterized, and being consulted before a change is announced meaningfully improves adoption later. This step is skipped constantly and it is a mistake.

**Cost modeling.** Each recommended opportunity gets a full cost estimate, build or configuration effort, per-seat licensing, training time, and ongoing maintenance. Licensing is the piece most audits under-state. Per-seat AI costs across a 40-person team add up to real annual money, and that number belongs next to the projected savings, not in a footnote.

**The deliverable itself.** A good audit document contains:

1. **Current-state time map.** Where hours go now, by role, with methodology stated.
2. **Ranked opportunity list.** Scored, with effort/impact/cost per item.
3. **Recommended sequence.** What to do first, second, third, and why, ordered by adoption risk, not just ROI.
4. **Explicit non-recommendations.** Things you might expect to be on the list that are not, with reasons. This section is the credibility test of the whole document.
5. **Data readiness assessment.** What needs cleanup before anything is built.
6. **Measurement plan.** How you will know in 90 days whether it worked, defined before you start.
7. **Total cost of ownership.** Consulting plus licensing plus internal time.

## What makes an audit good versus useless

**Good audits are portable.** You should be able to hand the document to a different vendor, or to your own internal team, and have it be immediately useful. If the recommendations only make sense if the auditing firm executes them, the audit was a sales proposal.

**Good audits say no.** A list where every idea is a good idea is not a prioritization. Expect at least a third of the surface area you might have expected to be ruled out.

**Good audits state methodology.** How many reps observed, over how long, with what sample. Findings without method are opinions with a table around them.

**Good audits quantify the null case.** What happens if you do nothing? Sometimes the honest answer for a small team is "not much, for another year." A consultant willing to say that is worth keeping.

## What it should cost, and when it is worth it

For a sales or marketing organization of 10 to 75 people, expect **$3,000 to $8,000** and two to four weeks. Larger or multi-department scopes run higher.

It is worth it when:

- You have more than about ten people whose time you are trying to affect
- You have bought AI tools before and adoption did not stick
- Leadership disagrees internally about where the bottleneck is
- You are about to spend $25,000 or more and want the sequencing right

It is probably not worth it when:

- You have fewer than five people, in which case just start using the tools and see what happens
- You already know exactly what you want built and only need someone to build it
- Nobody in leadership has capacity to act on the findings for the next two quarters

That last one is real. An audit that sits in a drive folder is worse than no audit, because it burns credibility with the team that participated in it.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is an AI audit?**
A structured assessment of where a team's time actually goes, which activities AI can meaningfully improve, and what changing them would cost. It produces a ranked plan, not a tool recommendation.

**How long does an AI audit take?**
Two to four weeks for a team of 10 to 75 people. Week one observation, week two analysis, weeks three and four validation and delivery.

**Will my team resist being observed?**
Some will, and how you frame it determines a lot. Framing it as "we're finding out which parts of your week we can give back to you" lands very differently than "we're evaluating efficiency." Both are true; only one gets you honest data.

**Do I have to use the same firm to implement?**
No, and you should be skeptical of any audit structured so that you effectively do. A portable deliverable is the mark of an honest engagement.

**What if the audit says AI won't help much?**
That is a valid and occasionally correct finding, and it is worth the fee. Knowing not to spend $40,000 is the same value as knowing where to spend it.
