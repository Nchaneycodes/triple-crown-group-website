AI for Cincinnati Accounting Firms and Financial Services

Quick answer: Accounting firms and financial services companies can use AI for business development research, proposal drafting, client communication drafting, and internal knowledge search. The constraints are real: client financial data requires business-tier tools with appropriate contractual terms, records retention rules apply to AI-assisted client communications, and a licensed professional must review and take responsibility for anything client-facing. Start with business development, where none of those constraints bind.

Cincinnati has a substantial professional services base, regional and local CPA firms, wealth management practices, and financial services companies of every size. It is also a sector where the compliance questions genuinely determine what is possible, so those come first.

The constraint map

For CPA firms:

  • Professional standards require the licensed professional to be responsible for the work regardless of what produced the draft
  • Client confidentiality obligations under state accountancy rules and professional standards
  • Peer review considerations for how technology is used in engagements
  • Data security expectations for client financial information

For registered investment advisers and broker-dealers:

  • Advertising and communication rules apply to any client-facing material regardless of how it was drafted
  • Books and records requirements extend to AI-assisted communications. If it goes to a client, it is retained
  • Supervision obligations mean the firm must be able to review what its people are doing with these tools
  • Investment recommendations require advisor judgment and responsibility

For everyone:

  • Client financial data is among the most sensitive categories that exists
  • Consumer free-tier tools are not appropriate for any of it

None of this prohibits AI. It shapes where you start.

Use cases for CPA firms

1. Business development and proposal drafting

The problem: most firms are constrained on business development capacity, not demand. Proposals and RFP responses get written by partners at night, and prospect research rarely happens at all.

What AI does: drafts proposals from your service descriptions and prior submissions, produces prospect research briefs, and assembles qualification packages.

Why it is the right starting point: no client data, no professional standards exposure, and it addresses a real constraint. Safest and most immediately valuable.

2. Client communication drafting

The problem: engagement letters, deadline reminders, document requests, planning correspondence, and tax law change explanations, high-volume and repetitive.

What AI does: drafts from your templates and the specifics of the client situation.

The boundary: a licensed professional reviews everything, and anything constituting advice carries their responsibility. Generic client education content about a tax law change is lower risk; advice about a specific client's situation is not.

3. Internal research and knowledge search

The problem: research questions where the answer exists in guidance, prior firm work, or standards, and finding it takes hours.

What AI does: searches and summarizes. Purpose-built professional research platforms with AI features are more appropriate here than general assistants, because they cite authoritative sources.

The boundary: general-purpose AI assistants should not be used as authorities on tax or accounting treatment. They produce confident, plausible, occasionally wrong answers about technical guidance. Use them to orient; verify against the actual source, always.

4. Advisory service development

Many firms are expanding into advisory work, CFO services, transaction support, technology consulting. AI helps with the research and deliverable production behind those services, and firms with existing client trust are unusually well positioned to sell advisory work.

Use cases for wealth management and financial advisory

Meeting preparation and follow-up

Client review preparation (portfolio changes, life events noted previously, agenda items) and follow-up summaries.

The boundary: anything sent to a client is subject to communication rules and retention requirements. Build the retention into the workflow from day one rather than retrofitting it, because retrofitting records retention is painful.

Content and client education

Market commentary, educational material, and newsletter content. Subject to advertising and communication rules, so compliance review applies exactly as it would to human-written material. AI changes drafting speed, not the review requirement.

Prospect and center-of-influence research

Research on prospects and referral sources, attorneys, CPAs, business owners. No client data, straightforward value.

Internal operational drafting

Meeting notes, internal summaries, process documentation. Low risk, meaningful time savings.

What to keep away from AI

  • Investment recommendations without advisor judgment and responsibility
  • Tax positions without professional verification against authoritative sources
  • Anything sent to a client unreviewed
  • Client financial data in unapproved tools, the most common actual violation
  • Audit judgment and evidence evaluation
  • Suitability determinations

Governance requirements

Before any deployment:

  1. Written AI policy covering approved tools, prohibited data, review requirements, and retention.
  2. Records retention alignment. If AI-assisted communications go to clients, they fall under your existing retention obligations. Make sure the workflow captures them.
  3. Supervision procedures. For regulated firms, the ability to review what people are doing with these tools is a requirement, not a nice-to-have.
  4. Vendor contractual review. Data handling, training usage, retention, and security. In writing.
  5. Professional liability carrier conversation. Some carriers now ask about AI use in renewal applications. Having a documented policy is a materially better answer than not having one.

What this costs

Small CPA firm, 10 to 30 people. Business-tier licenses, BD and proposal workflow, policy, training. $10,000 to $22,000 year one.

Mid-sized regional firm, 50 to 200 people. The above plus client communication workflows, internal knowledge search, and firm-wide governance. $40,000 to $90,000.

RIA or wealth management practice, 10 to 40 people. Licenses, meeting prep and follow-up workflows, content workflow with compliance review integration, retention setup. $18,000 to $45,000.

Sequencing

Phase 1, Policy and training. Before tools. Your staff are already using AI with client information at most firms, and that is the actual current risk.

Phase 2, Business development and proposals. No client data, real value, builds confidence.

Phase 3, Client communication drafting with review and retention built in.

Phase 4, Internal research and knowledge search, using professional-grade platforms rather than general assistants for technical questions.

Frequently asked questions

Can accounting firms use AI? Yes, for business development, proposal drafting, client communication drafting, and internal search, with licensed review of anything client-facing and appropriate tools for client data.

Is AI allowed in financial advisory? Yes as a drafting and research assistant. Communication rules, retention requirements, and supervision obligations all continue to apply, and no recommendation is generated without advisor responsibility.

What is the safest first use case? Business development and proposal drafting. No client data, no professional standards exposure, real capacity constraint.

Can we use AI for tax research? Use professional research platforms with AI features that cite authoritative sources. General assistants are useful for orientation and unreliable as authorities, verify every technical position against the source.

What about client consent? Practice varies and expectations are evolving. Many firms are adding language to engagement letters describing technology use in service delivery. Discuss specifics with your professional liability carrier and counsel, but the direction of travel is toward more disclosure, not less.
